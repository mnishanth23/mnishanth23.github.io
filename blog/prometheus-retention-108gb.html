How Prometheus retention ate 108 GB, and the one-line fix

10 Sep 20265 min read

By Nishanth M · DevOps Engineer | SRE

A disk-space crisis on one of our backend servers didn’t come from logs, uploads or a runaway database. It came from monitoring: Prometheus was keeping a full year of metrics. Cutting retention to 90 days freed about 108 GB.

The fix was one flag. Understanding why it was the right flag, and how to stop it from happening again, took a bit longer. This post covers both.

The server and the symptom

I administer the backend server for our AI-SaaS product. It runs a fairly typical mix in Docker: Prometheus and cAdvisor for metrics, MongoDB (both 4 and 8), MariaDB, Redis and the PHP application. On a box like that, free disk is shared infrastructure. When it runs low, every service on it is at risk, not just the one that’s growing.

When I went looking for what was using the space, the biggest consumer was the Prometheus data volume at 127 GB. Retention was set to one year.

How Prometheus decides what to keep

Prometheus stores samples in its local TSDB. Incoming data is held in memory, protected by a write-ahead log (WAL), and written out as two-hour blocks. In the background, those blocks are compacted into larger ones covering up to 10% of the retention time, or 31 days, whichever is smaller.[1]

Two flags decide when old blocks are deleted:

  • --storage.tsdb.retention.time: how long to keep samples, for example 90d or 1y.
  • --storage.tsdb.retention.size: the maximum bytes of blocks to keep, for example 50GB. It’s disabled by default.

If you set both, whichever limit is hit first wins.[1] A few details matter in practice:

  • Deletion works on whole blocks. A block is removed only once it’s fully past the retention window, and cleanup runs in the background, so it can take up to two hours.
  • The size limit counts the WAL and memory-mapped head chunks, but only persistent blocks are deleted to stay under it.
  • These are command-line flags, not prometheus.yml settings. A config reload won’t pick them up; the process has to restart.[2]

The one-line fix

The change itself was retention from one year to 90 days. With Prometheus in Docker, that’s one line in the container’s command:

After the restart, Prometheus dropped every block older than 90 days. The volume went from 127 GB to 14 GB, freeing about 108 GB, and the disk crisis was over.

Why not just add disk? Because the growth would have come back. With a year of retention, the volume only stops growing once the oldest block expires, and then it tracks whatever the ingestion rate does. A bigger disk buys time; a retention number you’ve thought about fixes the cause.

Checking the result doesn’t take long. The size of the data directory is the obvious check. Inside Prometheus, the TSDB Status page in the web UI shows head-block statistics, and a range query against any long-lived series should now start about 90 days back. Remember the two-hour cleanup window: if nothing shrinks right after the restart, wait before assuming the flag didn’t take.

Before you shorten retention

The data is deleted, not archived. If anyone might need older metrics, take a TSDB snapshot first. The admin API’s /api/v1/admin/tsdb/snapshot endpoint does this, but only when Prometheus runs with --web.enable-admin-api.[3] Then turn the admin API back off.

Sizing retention instead of guessing

“A year” and “90 days” are both guesses until you put numbers on them. The Prometheus docs give a rough capacity formula:[1]

disk ≈ retention (seconds) × samples ingested per second × 1–2 bytes per sample

Retention is linear in that formula, but ingestion isn’t fixed. cAdvisor exports series per container,[11] and series come and go as containers are created and replaced. Two habits follow from this. First, decide retention from how far back people actually query, not from how much history feels safe. Second, set --storage.tsdb.retention.size as a safety net under the time limit. The docs suggest at most 80–85% of the disk allocated to Prometheus, leaving room for compaction, which briefly keeps old and new blocks on disk together.[1] With both set, Prometheus trims data before the disk fills, even if ingestion grows faster than you planned.

When you really do need a year

Sometimes long-term trends are worth keeping for capacity planning or year-over-year comparisons. Raw, full-resolution local retention is the most expensive way to get them. Better options:

  • Recording rules precompute the aggregates you chart, such as per-service CPU or request rates.[4][5] On their own they don’t save space, because recorded series live in the same TSDB under the same retention. They pay off when a second, long-retention Prometheus keeps only those aggregates, pulled through federation.[6]
  • Remote storage moves history off the box. Prometheus can remote-write to systems such as Thanos or Grafana Mimir.[7] Thanos’ compactor also downsamples old data to 5-minute and 1-hour resolution, which is what you want for a year-long graph anyway.[8][9]

Either way, local Prometheus goes back to doing what it’s good at: fast queries over recent data for dashboards and alerting.

What I took away

  1. Retention is a capacity decision. Treat it like any other disk budget, with a number you can defend.
  2. Set a size limit as well as a time limit. Time says what you want to keep; size stops the disk from filling up.
  3. Monitor the monitoring. Prometheus exports its own storage metrics; graph them next to free disk.
  4. Keep long-term history somewhere built for it, at lower resolution, instead of keeping every raw sample locally.

Google’s SRE book makes the point that monitoring should stay simple, with data that is actually used.[10] A year of raw samples that nobody queried was the opposite of that, and it cost about 108 GB of disk on a shared server.
