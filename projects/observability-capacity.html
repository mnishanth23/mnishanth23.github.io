What I look after

01

The problem

Three separate jobs, one theme: keep the systems observable without the observability itself becoming the problem.

  • A disk-space crisis on the AI-SaaS backend server. The server runs everything in Docker on shared disk, and when space ran out the largest consumer turned out to be Prometheus, keeping a full year of metrics.
  • An old dev Elasticsearch. Dev Elasticsearch and Kibana were on 7.11.2. You can’t jump from there to 8.x; the supported path goes through the last 7.x release first.
  • Microservices without uptime alerts in the team’s chat. If a service went down, someone had to notice.

02

What I did

  1. Cut Prometheus retention from 1 year to 90 days, which freed about 108 GB: the volume went from 127 GB to 14 GB.
  2. Upgraded dev Elasticsearch and Kibana from 7.11.2 to 7.17.28, the required step before 8.x. The 8.x upgrade is next.
  3. Set up Uptime Kuma for our microservices, with Google Chat alert templates.
  4. Built the ELK Log Simulator, a public repo that generates logs, parses them with Fluent Bit and ships them to Elasticsearch.
  5. Keep administering the backend server: Prometheus, MongoDB 4 and 8, MariaDB, Redis, PHP and cAdvisor in Docker.

03

How it works

Finding where the disk went

On a host where everything runs in Docker, the usual suspects are images, logs and volumes. The quickest way in is to measure, not guess: check which filesystem is full, then which Docker volume is biggest. Here the answer was the Prometheus data volume.

Retention is a single flag

Prometheus keeps its time-series database on disk and deletes data older than its retention window. With a one-year window, every metric from every container was kept for a year. Ninety days still covers day-to-day troubleshooting and recent trends, and cutting the window let Prometheus drop the older blocks.

Trade-off

Shorter retention means you can’t look back more than 90 days. That’s a deliberate choice: if long-term trends are ever needed, the fix is to store them somewhere built for it, not to keep a year of raw samples on the application server’s disk.

Elasticsearch: 7.11.2 → 7.17.28, then 8.x

Elasticsearch only supports upgrading to 8.x from the last 7.x minor, so 7.17 is a required stop, not an optional one. It’s also the version that shows what 8.x will break: Kibana’s Upgrade Assistant lists deprecated settings and indices to fix first. Kibana moves with Elasticsearch, so both went to 7.17.28 together. I did it on dev; the 8.x upgrade is next.

Uptime Kuma with Google Chat alerts

Uptime Kuma checks each microservice on a schedule and sends a notification when its status changes. Alerts go to Google Chat through a webhook, using templates so every alert reads the same way: which service, what changed, and when. A consistent alert is faster to act on than a raw payload.

ELK Log Simulator (public)

To practise log pipelines without touching real systems, I built a small simulator: it generates log lines, Fluent Bit parses them into structured fields, and they land in Elasticsearch, ready to explore in Kibana. The code is on GitHub.

04

Results

  • About 108 GB freed: the Prometheus volume went from 127 GB to 14 GB after cutting retention from 1 year to 90 days.
  • Dev Elasticsearch and Kibana on 7.17.28, upgraded from 7.11.2. 8.x is next.
  • Uptime Kuma monitoring for microservices, with Google Chat alert templates.
  • A public ELK Log Simulator with Fluent Bit parsing and Elasticsearch.

05

What I’d do next

  • Upgrade dev to Elasticsearch 8.x: clear the Upgrade Assistant’s findings, take a snapshot, then upgrade Elasticsearch and Kibana together.
  • Alert on disk usage for the Prometheus volume itself, so storage is a warning on a dashboard, not a crisis.
  • If long-term trends become necessary, keep downsampled history in dedicated storage rather than raising raw retention again.
  • Publish a status page from Uptime Kuma so teams can check service health without asking.
